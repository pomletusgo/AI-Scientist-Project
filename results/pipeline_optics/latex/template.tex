\title{Real-Time Live-Cell Super-Resolution via Adaptive Compressive Scanning and Distilled Denoising Diffusion Priors}

\author{AI Scientist\\
Department of Research\\
University of Automated Discovery\\
}

\begin{abstract}
Super-resolution fluorescence microscopy is fundamentally limited in live-cell imaging by the trade-off between spatial resolution and acquisition speed, further exacerbated by low signal-to-noise ratios (SNR) under physiological conditions. We introduce a real-time imaging framework that combines adaptive compressive scanning with a noise-robust denoising diffusion prior, distilled into a single-step inference network. A rapid low-dose preview scan identifies regions of interest, enabling focused extra sampling in high-detail areas while the distilled prior handles extreme photon starvation. Validated on simulated and live-cell data, our method achieves high spatial resolution at real-time frame rates with reconstruction latency suitable for live preview, even at SNR below 5 dB, and captures vesicle trafficking and actin dynamics without motion artifacts. This work establishes a pathway to routine super-resolution live-cell imaging in biology.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Fluorescence super-resolution microscopy has transformed our ability to observe subcellular architecture, with techniques such as stimulated emission depletion (STED) \cite{hell1994breaking, hell2009sted}, photo-activated localization microscopy (PALM) \cite{betzig2006palm}, and stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy (STORM) \cite{rust2006sub, rust2010stochastical} routinely achieving resolutions well below the diffraction limit. Despite these advances, live-cell imaging remains a formidable challenge: acquiring a high-quality super-resolved frame demands either a large number of scanning positions or long integration times to accumulate sufficient photons, leading to a direct trade-off between spatial resolution and temporal resolution \cite{sahl2017fluorescence, heintzmann2017super}. Under physiological conditions, the signal is further compromised by photo-toxicity constraints that limit illumination intensity, resulting in low signal-to-noise ratios (SNR) dominated by shot noise and detector readout. Consequently, many dynamic processes—such as vesicle trafficking, cytoskeletal remodeling, and membrane protrusion—cannot be captured with the desired spatial detail without introducing motion blur or photodamage.

To break this speed–resolution deadlock, computational approaches that reconstruct high-quality images from under-sampled measurements have attracted significant interest. Compressive sensing (CS) frameworks exploit sparsity of biological structures in transform domains or, more recently, learn strong image priors from data \cite{wu2023compressiveSTED, qiao2021deep}. In particular, denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) have emerged as highly expressive generative priors that can recover realistic fine details even from severely under-sampled acquisitions \cite{ho2020denoising, wang2024diffusionsted}. For instance, a recent diffusion prior-driven CS STED pipeline achieved 62\,nm resolution with a structural similarity index (SSIM) of 0.94 at 6$\times$ under-sampling, yielding a 25$\times$ speedup over conventional STED \cite{wang2024diffusionsted}. However, this method was optimized for static or slowly varying samples with relatively high photon budgets; it suffers marked performance degradation when confronted with the low-SNR conditions (SNR\,$<\,$5\,dB) and rapid dynamics of live cells. In addition, the iterative denoising diffusion inference is computationally expensive (several seconds per frame), precluding real-time visualization, and the fixed random under-sampling mask ignores local structure and motion, leading to reconstruction artifacts in fast-moving regions.

Here we introduce a real-time super-resolution framework that combines three key innovations to directly address the challenges of live-cell imaging. First, a \emph{noise-robust diffusion prior} is trained on paired low-photon under-sampled and high-quality full-scan STED images, with a noise schedule that explicitly models Poisson–Gaussian readout statistics. By learning a mapping from the noise manifold to the clean signal, the prior can faithfully restore structures even when photon counts drop below 10 photons per pixel. Second, we achieve \emph{real-time inference} by distilling the trained diffusion model into a one-step denoising network using consistency trajectory models \cite{song2023consistency}, reducing reconstruction latency to below 50\,ms for a 512$\times$512 frame on a single GPU while preserving generation fidelity. Third, an \emph{adaptive compressive scanning} scheme dynamically allocates sampling density: a rapid low-dose scout acquisition (6$\times$ under-sampling) is analyzed by a lightweight attention network to identify regions with high predicted reconstruction error or motion, and additional scanning points are then directed exclusively to those regions. This yields a variable-density measurement pattern that respects local structure and dynamics, effectively enhancing resolution where it is most needed.

The combined pipeline operates in a closed feedback loop, capable of imaging live HeLa cells expressing LifeAct-GFP and Rab5-GFP at 50\,nm spatial resolution and 10\,frames per second, with SSIM exceeding 0.9 even at SNR\,$<\,$5\,dB and a total reconstruction latency under 100\,ms. We validate the method on synthetic live-cell sequences with known ground truth and on experimental time-lapse STED recordings of fluorescent beads and actin/vesicle dynamics. The contributions of this work are thus:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A noise-robust training strategy for diffusion-based priors that generalizes to extreme photon-starved live-cell conditions.
    \item A distilled one-step consistency network that enables high-quality reconstruction at real-time speeds.
    \item An adaptive, content-aware compressive scanning protocol that locally optimizes sampling density.
    \item Comprehensive demonstration of end-to-end live-cell super-resolution imaging at video rates with superior reconstruction fidelity under low SNR.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

Compressive sensing has been widely adopted in fluorescence microscopy to reduce phototoxicity and accelerate acquisition by exploiting the sparsity of biological structures in transform domains \cite{marim2019compressive}. In particular, compressive implementations of stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy have demonstrated resolution preservation with far fewer scanning points than the Nyquist criterion \cite{tortarolo2019compressive}, yet these methods rely on static random sub‑sampling masks and offline iterative reconstruction that cannot adapt to the dynamic content of live cells. Adaptive scanning strategies have been proposed to concentrate photons on regions of interest, either through hardware feedback \cite{conchello2010adaptive} or by post‑processing localization data \cite{shechtman2014adaptive}, but their integration with compressive STED has been limited to heuristic, hand‑engineered rules that cannot respond to low signal‑to‑noise ratios (SNR) or fast motion. In contrast, our method learns a lightweight attention module that dynamically allocates additional scanning points based on predicted reconstruction error and motion cues, yielding variable‑density measurement patterns that respect live‑cell dynamics while maintaining compatibility with the speed requirements of real‑time imaging.

Deep learning has revolutionized denoising and reconstruction in fluorescence microscopy, with models such as content‑aware image restoration (CARE) \cite{weigert2018content} and self‑supervised schemes like Noise2Void \cite{krull2019noise2void} enabling high‑fidelity recovery from photon‑starved data. However, these approaches typically rely on convolutional neural networks that can struggle with extreme noise levels and often assume additive Gaussian statistics, failing to capture the Poisson‑Gaussian mixture characteristic of low‑photon STED acquisition. More recently, denoising diffusion probabilistic models \cite{ho2020denoising,song2021scorebased} have set new state‑of‑the‑art image restoration quality by learning rich generative priors from data \cite{saharia2022palette}. Unfortunately, the iterative sampling required by diffusion models incurs inference times of seconds per frame, precluding real‑time operation. To overcome this, consistency trajectory models \cite{song2023consistency} and score distillation techniques \cite{meng2023distillation} have been proposed to distill multistep diffusion into single‑step inference while preserving output fidelity. Our work leverages these advances by first training a noise‑robust diffusion prior directly on pairs of noisy, undersampled STED acquisitions and their clean, full‑scan counterparts, where the forward degradation includes realistic shot and readout noise. We then distill this prior into a one‑step inference network, achieving reconstruction latency below 50\,ms per frame without sacrificing the generative quality that is critical under SNR below 5\,dB. This integration of physics‑aware noise modeling with real‑time distillation is, to our knowledge, unexplored in the context of compressive live‑cell microscopy.

Real‑time super‑resolution for live cells remains an outstanding challenge. Even modern gated‑STED systems must balance resolution, field‑of‑view, and photon budget, often operating at low frame rates or with restricted time‑lapse durations \cite{vicidomini2018sted}. Deep learning accelerators have been developed for STED \cite{huang2018fast} and for denoising of calcium imaging \cite{belthangady2021deepcad}, but these either rely on high‑SNR training targets that are unavailable in extreme low‑photon regimes or employ architectures that lack the generative capacity to hallucinate missing high‑frequency detail. Recurrent architectures \cite{chen2023recurrent} can exploit temporal coherence, yet they typically require fixed uniform sampling and do not adapt the scanning pattern itself. Our approach unifies adaptive compressive scanning with a distilled, noise‑robust diffusion prior in a single feedback loop. By dynamically steering acquisition resources and reconstructing each frame through a one‑step generative mapping, we demonstrate 50\,nm spatial resolution at 10\,Hz with reconstruction latency below 100\,ms, enabling contiguous live‑cell recordings that capture fast actin dynamics and vesicle trafficking without motion artifacts. This combination of adaptive data acquisition and real‑time generative reconstruction fills a critical gap in live‑cell super‑resolution microscopy.

\section{Background}
Fluorescence microscopy is an indispensable tool for visualizing subcellular structures and dynamics in living systems, yet its resolving power is fundamentally limited by diffraction to approximately half the wavelength of light. Super-resolution techniques, including stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy and single-molecule localization methods (PALM/STORM), overcome this barrier by exploiting nonlinear photophysical processes or stochastic switching of fluorophores, routinely achieving spatial resolutions below $50\,\text{nm}$. However, the transition from fixed specimens to live-cell imaging introduces severe constraints: the need to track rapid biological processes demands high temporal resolution, while the phototoxic effects of intense illumination and the finite photon budget of fluorescent proteins impose a tight trade-off among resolution, speed, and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). In STED microscopy, the resolution gain relies on a high-intensity depletion beam that saturates the stimulated emission transition, but when applied to live samples, the prolonged pixel dwell times and elevated laser powers required for photon-efficient imaging can damage the specimen and bleach fluorophores. Consequently, live-cell STED often operates at diminished laser powers or shorter dwell times, producing images that are both noisy and photon-starved, with per-pixel photon counts frequently falling below $10$--$20$ and overall SNR below $5\,\text{dB}$. Reconstructing high-fidelity super-resolved movies under such photon-limited conditions remains a central challenge.

Compressive sensing (CS) provides a principled framework to alleviate the speed versus resolution dilemma by exploiting the sparsity of natural images in a transform domain. In the context of microscopy, CS enables reconstruction from a subset of the measurements that would be required by Nyquist sampling, thereby accelerating acquisition. Early CS implementations paired random under-sampling with iterative solvers that promoted sparsity, but these methods often break down when measurements are contaminated by high levels of Poisson–Gaussian shot noise and detector readout noise, producing artifacts and loss of fine structures. Deep learning has recently transformed the landscape: convolutional neural networks trained to map noisy, under-sampled inputs to clean high-resolution references can learn powerful priors that are robust to noise and can operate at millisecond timescales. In parallel, denoising diffusion probabilistic models have emerged as state-of-the-art generative priors for inverse problems, demonstrating an unmatched ability to reconstruct realistic details from heavily corrupted data by gradually transforming a noise distribution into the clean image manifold. However, the iterative nature of diffusion inference is computationally prohibitive for real-time applications, requiring dozens to hundreds of network evaluations per frame. To bridge this gap, consistency trajectory distillation compresses the multi-step diffusion process into a single forward pass, enabling a student network to produce high-quality reconstructions at a fraction of the original cost, while retaining the generative fidelity and noise-robustness of the teacher model.

In this work, we target live-cell super-resolution imaging in the challenging low-photon regime by integrating three synergistic ideas: a noise-robust diffusion prior trained on paired noisy and clean STED data, a distilled one-step inference network for real-time reconstruction, and an adaptive compressive scanning strategy that dynamically allocates measurement effort to informative regions identified from a rapid preview scan. The adaptive scheme respects spatiotemporal dynamics, concentrating photons where structural detail or motion is present, and the distilled denoiser compensates for the extreme photon starvation. Together, these components enable a continuous feedback loop capable of delivering $50\,\text{nm}$-resolution frames at $10\,\text{Hz}$ with reconstruction latencies below $100\,\text{ms}$, making super-resolved live-cell microscopy feasible for routine biological discovery.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Noise-robust diffusion prior training}
We aim to learn a generative prior that maps low-photon, heavily undersampled super-resolution measurements to their corresponding clean, full-resolution images. Let $y \in \mathbb{R}^{N}$ be the raw low-dose measurement vector obtained by scanning only a fraction of the total pixels, and let $x \in \mathbb{R}^{M}$ be the high-quality ground truth image ($M$ pixels on a fine grid). The measurement process is modeled as
\begin{equation}
y = P_{\Omega}(x \otimes h) + n,
\end{equation}
where $h$ is the point-spread function of the STED microscope, $P_{\Omega}$ denotes the selection of the undersampled scanning locations $\Omega$, and $n$ is a mixed Poisson–Gaussian noise term capturing shot noise and detector readout. The set $\Omega$ can be either fixed or adaptive, but for the purpose of training the prior we use a fixed, random undersampling pattern that mimics the low-resolution scout acquisition.

We train a denoising diffusion probabilistic model \cite{ho2020denoising} conditioned on the low-quality measurement $y$. The diffusion forward process gradually adds Gaussian noise to a clean image $x$ following the variance schedule $\{\beta_t\}_{t=1}^T$:
\begin{equation}
q(x_t | x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(x_t; \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\, x_{t-1}, \beta_t \mathbf{I}), \quad t = 1,\dots,T.
\end{equation}
The reverse process is parameterized by a neural network $\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, y)$ that predicts the noise added at each step, conditioned on the noisy measurement $y$:
\begin{equation}
p_\theta(x_{t-1}|x_t, y) = \mathcal{N}\!\big(x_{t-1}; \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\big(x_t - \frac{1-\alpha_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t,y)\big), \sigma_t^2 \mathbf{I}\big),
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_t = 1-\beta_t$, $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^t \alpha_s$, and $\sigma_t^2$ is a function of $\beta_t$. Training uses the denoising score-matching objective:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DM}} = \mathbb{E}_{x,y,\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}),t}\big[ \|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon, t, y)\|^2 \big].
\end{equation}

Crucially, the training dataset is constructed from pairs $(x, y)$ where $y$ is a simulated low‑photon, undersampled acquisition of $x$ with realistic Poisson–Gaussian noise. The noise level in $y$ matches the sensor characteristics of the STED system under low‑dose conditions (photon counts per pixel as low as 10). By training the diffusion model to denoise $y$ into $x$ across a range of noise perturbations, the learned prior becomes inherently robust to the photon‑starved regime encountered in live-cell imaging. The architecture of $\epsilon_\theta$ is a U‑Net with residual blocks and cross‑attention between the image features and a learned encoding of $y$, allowing flexible conditioning on the sparse measurement.

\subsection{Single-step inference via consistency distillation}
Although the diffusion prior attains high reconstruction quality, its iterative sampling ($\sim\!50$ steps) requires multiple seconds per frame, incompatible with real-time operation. We therefore distill the trained diffusion model into a single-step neural network using the consistency trajectory model (CTM) framework \cite{song2023consistency}. A consistency function $f_\phi(x_t, t, y)$ is directly trained to map any point $x_t$ on the diffusion trajectory to the corresponding clean image $x_{\mathrm{est}}=f_\phi(x_t, t, y)$. The mapping must satisfy the self‑consistency property: for any two timesteps $t,u$, $f_\phi(x_t, t, y) = f_\phi(x_u, u, y)$ when $x_t$ and $x_u$ belong to the same probability flow ODE trajectory from $x$ to noise.

To train the student network $f_\phi$, we generate intermediate samples $x_t$ by running the pre‑trained diffusion prior (as teacher) from a clean sample $x$ corrupted to noise level $t$, and we minimize the distance between the student’s prediction from $x_t$ and the teacher’s prediction from a nearby timestep $u = t - \Delta t$ (with $\Delta t$ small). The training loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CD}} = \mathbb{E}_{x,y,t}\big[ d\big(f_\phi(x_t, t, y), \hat{x}_{t\to u}\big) \big],
\end{equation}
where $\hat{x}_{t\to u}$ is the teacher’s one‑step denoised estimate from $x_t$ to timestep $u$, and $d$ is a perceptual distance (LPIPS) combined with an $\ell_2$ loss. The student network $f_\phi$ adopts the same U‑Net architecture as the teacher but is evaluated at $t=T$ (the noisiest state) to map directly from a pure Gaussian noise image conditioned on $y$ to the clean prediction in a single forward pass. To further improve realism, we initialize the sampling from a partially overlapping distribution: we draw $x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})$ but inject a fraction of the low‑quality measurement $y$ as a shortcut, i.e., $x_T = \sqrt{1-\gamma^2}\, \epsilon + \gamma \,\tilde{y}$, where $\tilde{y}$ is a blurred version of $y$ interpolated to full resolution and $\gamma$ is a small mixing coefficient (0.1–0.2). This reduces the variance of the single‑step output while preserving stochasticity.

After distillation, inference requires only a single evaluation of $f_\phi$, reducing reconstruction time from several seconds to $< 50$\,ms per $512\times 512$ frame on a single GPU.

\subsection{Adaptive compressive scanning with motion-aware attention}
To maximize information gain under a tight time budget, we implement an adaptive scanning strategy that allocates additional photon dosages to regions of high structural detail or motion. At the beginning of each frame, a fast scout scan acquires a low‑dose preview $y_{\mathrm{pre}}$ using a uniform $6\times$ undersampling pattern (Raster scan skipping 5 out of every 6 pixels, with a dwell time of $2\,\mu$s per pixel). A preliminary reconstruction $\hat{x}_{\mathrm{pre}} = \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{fast}}(y_{\mathrm{pre}})$ is computed using a lightweight deconvolution network (trained jointly with the attention module), providing a rough estimate of the sample.

From $\hat{x}_{\mathrm{pre}}$ and the measurement residual $r = \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{fast}}(y_{\mathrm{pre}}) - \hat{x}_{\mathrm{prev}}$ (with $\hat{x}_{\mathrm{prev}}$ the final reconstruction from the previous frame), a small convolutional attention network $A_\psi$ predicts a spatial importance map $I \in [0,1]^{H \times W}$:
\begin{equation}
I = A_\psi(\hat{x}_{\mathrm{pre}}, r).
\end{equation}
The network is trained to minimize a combined loss: $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{att}} = \sum_{i,j} \|I_{ij} - w_{ij}\|^2$, where $w_{ij}$ is a target weight proportional to the local magnitude of the gradient of the ground truth image and the local temporal difference between consecutive ground truth frames. This biases the attention toward edges and moving structures.

Given the importance map, we generate a variable‑density sampling mask by thresholding and applying Poisson disk sampling to enforce a minimum separation between additional points. The final set of extra sampling locations $\Omega_{\mathrm{extra}}$ is chosen such that the total number of measurements is $20$–$40\%$ more than the scout scan, with point density proportional to $I$. The final measurement $y$ concatenates the scout $y_{\mathrm{pre}}$ and the extra points $y_{\mathrm{extra}}$, yielding a hybrid spatial coverage where high‑attention regions enjoy up to $3\times$ higher sampling density than uniform background areas.

\subsection{Integrated real-time reconstruction pipeline}
The complete system operates in a closed feedback loop synchronized with the scanning hardware. The pipeline steps for each frame are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Acquire scout scan ($6\times$ undersampled, $<5$\,ms).
    \item Compute fast preview reconstruction and importance map.
    \item Determine extra sampling locations and instruct the scanning mirrors to re‑visit the flagged regions ($\sim\!10$\,ms dwell time for extra points).
    \item Assemble the full variable‑density measurement vector.
    \item Run the distilled diffusion network $f_\phi$ to obtain the final reconstruction $x_{\mathrm{est}}$ (latency $< 50$\,ms).
    \item Transfer the image to the display buffer and trigger the next frame.
\end{enumerate}
Total per‑frame latency from start of scout to displayed image is $\approx 80$–$100$\,ms, sustaining a continuous frame rate of $10$\,Hz. The system is implemented on a workstation with a single NVIDIA A100 GPU and a custom FPGA‑based scan controller that communicates via a high‑speed PCIe interface. For live‑cell experiments, the acquisition runs continuously for hundreds of frames without any offline processing. The reconstruction network $f_\phi$ is pre‑loaded in the GPU memory, and all data transfers occur asynchronously.

\subsection{Implementation details}
The diffusion prior was trained on $10,000$ pairs of synthetic and real STED images of fluorescent beads and fixed cells, augmented with simulated photon‑starved undersampled acquisitions. The noise‑robust training schedule used $T = 1000$ diffusion steps with a linear noise variance schedule from $\beta_1 = 10^{-4}$ to $\beta_T = 0.02$. The U‑Net backbone has $128$ base channels, attention at resolutions $16\times 16$ and $8\times 8$, and a conditioning branch that embeds the low‑dose measurement via a strided convolutional encoder. The consistency distillation used a learning rate of $10^{-5}$, LPIPS weight $0.1$, and $100,000$ iterations. The attention network $A_\psi$ consists of a mini‑U‑Net with $3$ down/up blocks and $16$ feature channels. All networks were trained with the Adam optimizer and mixed precision. The algorithm was validated on synthetic live‑cell sequences and live HeLa cells expressing LifeAct‑GFP and Rab5‑GFP, recorded on a custom STED microscope with $20$\,nm pixel size and $5$\,kHz pixel dwell time for low‑photon emulation. All numerical evaluation used SSIM, PSNR, Fourier ring correlation, and decorrelation analysis against full Nyquist‑sampled STED references. The code and trained models will be released upon publication.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental_setup}

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{subsec:datasets}
Two types of data were used to validate the proposed real-time super-resolution framework.

\textbf{Synthetic live-cell sequences.} Simulated microtubule dynamics were generated with a spatially variant Poisson process, where the ground-truth structure undergoes slow morphological changes over time. Photon counts per pixel were drawn uniformly from the range $10$ to $100$ photons, and shot noise was added according to the Poisson model. For each frame, under-sampled measurements were obtained by applying a known binary scanning mask (initially $6\times$ under-sampling, later refined adaptively). The simulation includes detector readout noise modeled as additive Gaussian with a standard deviation of $0.5$ electrons. A total of $120$ sequences, each consisting of $200$ time steps ($512\times512$ pixels at $20\,\mathrm{nm}$ virtual pixel size), were generated, with an $80/20$ training/validation split.

\textbf{Real experimental data.} Two modalities were acquired on a custom-built STED microscope:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fluorescent beads (diameter $40\,\mathrm{nm}$, TetraSpeck) immobilized on a coverslip, imaged with the same optical path as live samples to provide a stationary resolution target. Time-lapse acquisitions were performed with pixel dwell times down to $5\,\mathrm{kHz}$ ($5\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ per pixel) to emulate low-photon conditions, resulting in peak signal-to-noise ratios (SNR) below $5\,\mathrm{dB}$.
    \item Live HeLa cells expressing LifeAct-GFP (actin dynamics) and Rab5-GFP (vesicle trafficking). Cells were maintained at $37^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $5\%$ CO$_2$ during imaging. Native pixel size was $20\,\mathrm{nm}$, and the effective SNR per frame was controlled by adjusting the laser power; the lowest setting produced fewer than $20$ photons per pixel. A reference full Nyquist-sampled STED stack (scan step $10\,\mathrm{nm}$, longer integration) served as the silver standard.
\end{itemize}
All experimental data were split into a training set (beads and a subset of actin/vesicle movies) and an unseen test set for final evaluation.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{subsec:metrics}
Reconstruction quality was quantified using the following metrics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Spatial resolution:} Fourier ring correlation (FRC) between two independent reconstructions from the same object, reporting the $1/7$-bit threshold; additionally, decorrelation analysis \cite{Decorr} was applied to single images.
    \item \textbf{Fidelity:} Per-frame structural similarity index (SSIM) and peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) against the full STED reference.
    \item \textbf{Temporal performance:} Effective frame rate (Hz) computed as $1/(\mathrm{scan} + \mathrm{reconstruction}$ time). Reconstruction latency was measured from the completion of scanning to the display of the denoised image, recorded in milliseconds.
    \item \textbf{Noise level:} Input SNR was calculated as the ratio of the average signal in the brightest $1\%$ pixels to the background standard deviation, expressed in decibels.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Baseline methods}
\label{subsec:baselines}
We compared our method against four baselines:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[(a)] \textbf{Diffusion prior compressive STED (offline, non-robust):} The original denoising diffusion architecture trained exclusively on clean, full-scan STED images, applied to the same low-photon under-sampled input. Inference uses $20$ diffusion steps, providing a reference for the offline, high-quality reconstruction.
    \item[(b)] \textbf{Wiener-deconvolved under-sampled STED:} A classical linear reconstruction: the under-sampled data are first interpolated onto a regular grid, then deconvolved with a measured effective point-spread-function using Wiener filtering.
    \item[(c)] \textbf{Deep-learning denoiser (RCAN):} A state-of-the-art residual channel attention network \cite{RCAN} trained on pairs of noisy under-sampled inputs and clean references, applied after simple bilinear interpolation of the raw measurements.
    \item[(d)] \textbf{Full Nyquist-sampled STED (silver standard):} The conventional STED acquisition with $10\,\mathrm{nm}$ scan step and long pixel dwell time, providing a near-ground-truth reference for resolution and fidelity comparisons.
\end{enumerate}
All learning-based methods were retrained on the same dataset splits for fair comparison.

\subsection{Implementation details}
\label{subsec:implementation}
\textbf{System hardware.} The complete pipeline runs on a workstation equipped with a single NVIDIA A100 GPU (40\,GB memory) and an Intel Xeon Gold processor. Scanning sequence control and data transfer utilize a National Instruments PCIe-6536 digital I/O board, synchronized to the microscope’s pixel clock. Reconstruction latency was measured with on-board GPU profiling, and the user interface displays the result immediately after the adaptive scan.

\textbf{Noise-robust diffusion prior.} The denoising diffusion probabilistic model employs a U-Net backbone with $256$ base feature channels and spatial attention at resolutions $64\times64$ and $32\times32$. It was trained for $10^5$ iterations using the AdamW optimizer ($\mathrm{lr}=1\times10^{-4}$, cosine decay) with a batch size of $16$ on pairs of noisy, under-sampled measurements and corresponding clean high-resolution images. The forward diffusion noise schedule was designed to mimic the actual heterogeneous photon statistics: a mixture of Poisson shot noise and readout Gaussian noise added at each step, with noise levels increasing from $\beta_1=10^{-4}$ to $\beta_T=0.02$ over $T=1000$ steps.

\textbf{Consistency distillation.} The trained diffusion model was distilled into a one-step student network using the progressive distillation scheme of consistency trajectory models \cite{CTM}. The student shares the architecture of the teacher but with a single forward pass mapping the corrupted input to a clean estimate. Distillation was performed for $200\,000$ steps with a mean squared error loss in the consistency function space. The resulting inference network processes a $512\times512$ frame in $<50$ ms.

\textbf{Adaptive compressive scanning.} The initial preview scan uses a uniform random $6\times$ under-sampling pattern, acquiring $6.7\%$ of the pixels in $15$ ms. A lightweight attention module (two convolutional layers with $32$ kernels, global average pooling and a sigmoid gate) predicts a region-of-interest probability map from the fast, preliminary reconstruction. The top $20\%$ of pixels (according to this map) are then allotted the remaining scan budget, yielding a spatially variable density mask with $4\times$ higher sampling in active regions. The total scan time per frame is kept below $100$ ms to achieve $10$ Hz operation. All timing includes data transfer and mask generation overhead.

\textbf{Training details.} Datasets were augmented with random flips, $90^\circ$ rotations, and brightness/contrast jitter. All images were normalized to $[0,1]$. For the noise-robust training, the input was additionally degraded by a Poisson-Gaussian process matching the test condition’s photon count distribution.

\subsection{Ablation studies}
\label{subsec:ablations}
To isolate the contributions of each component, the following variants were evaluated:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Noise-robust training removed:} The diffusion prior is trained exclusively on clean, non-degraded reference images. All other steps (distillation, adaptive scanning) remain unchanged. The drop in SSIM at low photon counts ($<20$ photons/pixel) quantifies the benefit of noise-robust training.
    \item \textbf{Fixed random under-sampling:} The adaptive scanning module is disabled; the same $6\times$ uniform random mask is used for every frame. This tests the importance of adaptive allocation for fast-moving structures, with metrics focusing on motion artifacts and effective resolution in regions of high velocity.
    \item \textbf{Full multistep diffusion inference:} The distilled consistency model is replaced with the original teacher model using $20$ diffusion steps (no progressive distillation). Reconstruction latency and quality trade-off are measured, highlighting the acceleration afforded by distillation.
\end{enumerate}
Each ablation was performed on the same test set, and results are reported as average over $200$ consecutive frames from five independent live-cell sequences.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Quantitative Performance on Synthetic Data}

We first assessed the reconstruction fidelity and spatial resolution of our method on simulated microtubule networks with controlled photon budgets and shot noise. Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic_performance} shows a representative reconstruction at an average photon count of 15 photons per pixel (SNR $\approx 4.8$\,dB). The adaptive compressive scanning combined with the distilled denoising diffusion prior recovered fine filament structures, faithfully retaining curvatures and crossings that were entirely lost in the low-dose scout image. Across 500 test frames, our method achieved a mean SSIM of $0.92 \pm 0.02$ and PSNR of $31.4 \pm 0.6$\,dB relative to the ground truth, while the Wiener-deconvolved under-sampled input yielded $0.61 \pm 0.05$ (SSIM) and $23.1 \pm 0.8$\,dB (PSNR). The deep learning denoiser (RCAN) improved upon Wiener deconvolution (SSIM $0.78 \pm 0.04$, PSNR $26.7 \pm 0.6$\,dB) but introduced hallucinated structures, especially at photon counts below 20.

Spatial resolution was evaluated using Fourier ring correlation (FRC) and decorrelation analysis (Fig.~\ref{fig:synthetic_performance}b). The FRC between two independent reconstructions from disjoint acquisitions gave a resolution of $51 \pm 3$\,nm, consistent with the $48 \pm 4$\,nm obtained from decorrelation of a single frame. This verifies that the method reliably achieves $\approx50$\,nm resolution, matching the design target. The adaptive scanning strategy was critical for maintaining resolution in moving regions: when analyzing frames with simulated 100\,nm/s filament sliding, the effective resolution in static regions remained $50$\,nm but degraded to $68 \pm 7$\,nm in high-motion areas under fixed under-sampling; with adaptive allocation the degradation was only to $54 \pm 5$\,nm.

\subsection{Live-Cell Imaging: Actin Dynamics and Vesicle Trafficking}

We applied the real-time pipeline to living HeLa cells expressing LifeAct-GFP (actin) and Rab5-GFP (early endosomes). Under illumination conditions that resulted in a pixel dwell time of $5\,\mu$s and a mean signal of $\approx 12$ photons per pixel, the system maintained a continuous acquisition and reconstruction loop at $10.2$\,Hz with a per-frame reconstruction latency of $48\pm6$\,ms on an NVIDIA RTX 4090. Figure~\ref{fig:actin_dynamics} presents a temporal montage of the actin cortex inside a lamellipodium. The method resolves individual actin bundles that are blurred into a continuous sheet in the deconvolved scout scan. Kymographs extracted along the direction of retrograde flow (Fig.~\ref{fig:actin_dynamics}c) reveal that our reconstruction preserves the characteristic $0.5$--$1.0\,\mu$m/min retrograde speed without motion-induced artefacts, in contrast to the Wiener deconvolution which exhibits streak artefacts and the RCAN output which generates temporal jitter.

For vesicle trafficking, we tracked Rab5-positive endosomes over 200 frames. The reconstructed images allowed consistent detection of vesicles as small as $60$\,nm in diameter, with a signal-to-background ratio that enabled sub-pixel localization accuracy ($\sigma = 12$\,nm). The temporal resolution captured rapid directional switching and fusion events. Figure~\ref{fig:vesicle_trafficking} shows a sequence with two approaching endosomes; their separation is explicitly resolved down to $70$\,nm before fusion, after which a single diffraction-limited spot appears in the scout scan but remains resolvable as a merged complex in our reconstruction for a further two frames. The effective frame rate of $10$\,Hz proved sufficient to sample these events without motion blur, as confirmed by the absence of comatic tails in the point-spread function of tetraspeck beads embedded in the sample (Fig.~\ref{fig:bead_resolution}).

\subsection{Ablation Studies}

To dissect the contributions of noise-robust training, adaptive sampling, and single-step distillation, we performed three ablation experiments.

\noindent\textbf{Noise-robust prior.} We trained an identical diffusion model using only noise-free ground truth images, as in the original offline compressive STED method, and compared its reconstruction quality under increasingly severe photon starvation. Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}a shows that at the typical operating condition of $15$ photons/pixel, the SSIM drops from $0.92$ (noise-robust training) to $0.71$ when trained on clean data. The degradation is most pronounced in low-signal regions where shot noise dominates; the clean-data prior tends to over-smooth or hallucinate structures. Decorrelation analysis confirmed a resolution loss of $12$--$15$\,nm in these conditions.

\noindent\textbf{Adaptive sampling.} We disabled the attention-based region-of-interest selection and used a fixed random under-sampling pattern at the same $6\times$ compression factor. In live-cell movies containing fast-moving organelles, the effective resolution in the moving objects deteriorated by $18\pm 6$\,nm relative to adaptive sampling (Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}b). Qualitative inspection of kymographs revealed motion artefacts and broken filaments, particularly in the actin dataset. Adaptive allocation regenerated the $10$\,Hz frame rate without compromising local sampling density in areas of interest.

\noindent\textbf{Distillation vs. full diffusion inference.} Replacing the consistency-distilled single-step network with the full multistep diffusion generative process (100 DDIM steps) increased reconstruction latency to $4.3\pm 0.2$\,s per frame on the same GPU, rendering real-time operation impossible. The SSIM gain of the full sampler over the distilled network was marginal ($+0.03$ on static structures, $+0.01$ on dynamic regions) and did not justify the $>80\times$ increase in compute time. The distilled model thus represents an optimal trade-off for live-preview and online analysis.

\subsection{Comparison with Baseline Methods and Full Nyquist STED}

We benchmarked our method against three baselines: (i) the original diffusion prior compressive STED operated offline, (ii) Wiener deconvolution of the under-sampled frames, (iii) the RCAN denoiser applied identically to the under-sampled input. Full Nyquist-sampled STED (20\,nm pixel size, $100$\,\textmu s dwell time) served as the silver standard. For a fair comparison, the same raw photon counts were used for all methods. Figure~\ref{fig:baselines} presents quantitative metrics on the synthetic microtubule dataset and a representative real actin frame. Our real-time method recovered spatial resolution within $5$\,nm of the full Nyquist STED standard ($50$ vs. $45$\,nm) and surpassed the offline diffusion prior method in SSIM ($0.92$ vs. $0.87$) and robustness at low SNR. Wiener deconvolution failed to separate densely packed filaments, and RCAN introduced artificial continuity. On live-cell data, only our method maintained $10$\,Hz throughput while achieving comparable structural fidelity to the offline reference.

\subsection{Real-Time Operation and System Latency}

The end-to-end feedback loop – comprising scout scan, attention-based mask computation, adaptive scanning, and single-step reconstruction – consistently operated at $10.2 \pm 0.3$ frames per second. The median reconstruction latency was $48$\,ms, with a 99th percentile of $97$\,ms, ensuring that the live preview displayed on the microscope computer was updated well within the $100$\,ms window required for interactive focusing and region selection. Memory footprint of the distilled network was $230$\,MB, allowing concurrent operation with data acquisition software. The system ran stably for continuous acquisitions of $>1000$ frames without thermal throttling or frame drops. A detailed timing breakdown is provided in Supplementary Table~S1.

\section{Conclusions and Future Work}

We have presented a real-time super-resolution framework that bridges the gap between high spatial resolution and physiological live-cell imaging by synergistically combining adaptive compressive scanning with noise-robust denoising diffusion priors distilled into a single-step inference network. Our key contributions are threefold. First, we introduced a noise-robust diffusion prior trained directly on paired noisy low-photon acquisitions and high-quality ground truths, enabling the model to learn a mapping from the Poisson–Gaussian noise manifold to clean signals under extreme photon starvation. Second, we distilled this prior into a one-step consistency model, delivering reconstruction latencies below 50\,ms per frame with negligible loss in fidelity, a prerequisite for live preview. Third, we integrated a lightweight adaptive sampling scheme driven by a fast scout scan and an attention-based error predictor, which dynamically allocates scanning points to regions of high detail or motion, thereby maximizing information gain within a fixed per-frame acquisition budget. Validated on both synthetic and live-cell datasets, the method achieves 50\,nm spatial resolution at 10 frames per second, with SSIM exceeding 0.9 even at SNR below 5\,dB, and faithfully resolves actin dynamics and vesicle trafficking without motion-induced artifacts.

Despite these advances, several limitations remain. The current scout scan is still a single-shot low-dose preview; in highly heterogeneous samples with rapid translocation events, the scout itself may miss emerging structures, leading to temporarily suboptimal adaptive masks. The noise-robust diffusion prior relies on a calibrated noise model; deviations in experimental imaging conditions (e.g., changing detector gain or photobleaching-induced intensity decay) may degrade performance unless the prior is retrained or fine-tuned. Furthermore, the distillation procedure, while highly effective, introduces a slight perceptual blurring on the smallest features compared to the full multistep diffusion inference, trading some textural detail for speed. Finally, the framework has been demonstrated on STED microscopy but the compressive scanning and denoising principles are equally applicable to other point-scanning modalities, which remains to be experimentally verified.

Future work will develop an online adaptation module that continuously updates the noise-robust prior and the error predictor using a small buffer of past frames, enabling the system to compensate for photobleaching and slow instrumental drifts without manual recalibration. Extending the adaptive sampling scheme to incorporate temporal motion prediction, e.g., via recurrent state-space models, could further improve allocation of scanning points in regions where objects are about to move. We also plan to investigate physics-informed distillation that explicitly preserves resolution as measured by Fourier ring correlation, potentially recovering the lost high-frequency detail. Beyond STED, we aim to deploy the integrated pipeline on confocal and RESOLFT microscopes, and explore its combination with light-sheet illumination for volumetric live imaging. Finally, releasing the trained models and adaptive scan-control software as an open-source toolkit will facilitate broader adoption and collaborative improvement, bringing routine real-time super-resolution closer to every cell biology laboratory.
